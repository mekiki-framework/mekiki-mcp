T3 names two things that cannot be handed over: the **endorsement of a direction** and **answerability**. They are on **different layers**. The first is a *component* the learner has, on the capacity layer. The second is a *relation* the learner stands in, on the second-personal layer. The paper says the two layers run at the same time in teaching but must not be merged.

## 1. Endorsement of a direction (a component the learner has)

- **Term.** Section 2.2 defines *direction* as "what the learner is oriented toward." It has two states: a *candidate* direction, "an orientation entertained but not yet owned," and an *endorsed* direction, "the evaluative commitment that makes some problems theirs rather than merely assigned."
- **What cannot be handed over.** Section 2.4 says it is not the supply of directions: "Proposals are cheap." What cannot be handed over is **ownership**: "Direction is not installed. It crystallizes as the learner's own, or it remains someone else's." Table 1 states it as "endorsement is non-substitutable."
- **Limit on distribution.** Section 2.5 says a hybrid human–AI system cannot "supply an individual learner's endorsement while the resulting judgment is still presented as that learner's own."
- **Still a component.** Section 4.1 says direction "resisted distribution, but it remained a component — something a learner comes to have."

## 2. Answerability (a relation the learner stands in)

- **Term.** Section 4.1 defines it as "the standing to have to answer for a judgment." It is the non-transferable relation "in which the warrant of a judgment remains anchored in a particular agent." The paper uses Darwall's sense: the standing to answer to someone who can demand an answer. It explicitly separates this from the Bakhtinian sense.
- **Why it is not a fourth component.** Finishing the sort "yields no further component." Answerability "is not a property of the learner at all. It is a relation between the learner and those entitled to ask." The paper marks the contrast in grammar: "one *has* a direction; one *stands in* answerability."
- **Sense in which it cannot be handed over.** The note under Table 1 in §2.4 says its "institutional allocation may change, but answering for a judgment cannot be performed by a substitute while the judgment remains the learner's own."
- **Not built into the sorting axis.** Section 4.2 argues that answerability was never among the things the sort ranked. It showed up only when the sorted capacities could not explain "who can be called to answer for a judgment."

## Are they on the same layer? No.

| | Endorsement of direction | Answerability |
|---|---|---|
| Kind | A component (property of the learner) | A relation (between the learner and those entitled to ask) |
| Verb | *has* | *stands in* |
| Place in the sort | In Table 1, as the one component that resists | Left out of Table 1 as "a relational status" |
| Layer | Layer one (capacity layer) | Layer two |
| Response | Encounter, dialogue, reflection, autonomy-supportive conditions; never direct installation | Protected second-personal exchange; the final addressee is a legitimate human |

- Section 5 says: "the layers — though pedagogically concurrent — must not be conflated." Layer one covers "everything the decomposition can place"; layer two covers "what layer one cannot reach."
- The abstract places the design conjecture for how direction crystallizes "within the capacity layer." The second layer is "protected second-personal exchange for what cannot [be distributed]."
- Section 4.1 warns that treating answerability as one more trait to foster "would repeat, at the level of theory, the conflation this paper exists to undo."

There is one wrinkle. The abstract describes layer one as "what can be distributed," yet endorsement itself cannot be distributed. Section 5 defines layer one as "everything the decomposition can place," and direction is placed in the sort. So endorsement belongs to layer one. The layers are divided by component versus relation, not by whether something can be handed over.
